# Agentic Commerce

Your in-app assistant already sees your store — sales, catalogue, the traffic funnel, business health. Agentic
commerce lets it act on what it sees. It can prepare the things it would otherwise only suggest — a gift card
for a lapsed customer, a promotion on slow-moving stock, a dynamic-pricing rule that targets a customer
segment — and it handles the reversible catalogue housekeeping outright.

Agentic commerce has a shopper side as well: the [Agent API](#shopping-assistants-the-agent-api) lets any
AI shopping assistant search a store, price a basket and hand a checkout to the shopper, described at the
end of this page.

Anything that moves money arrives as a **draft you approve** — a gift card, a promotion, a pricing
rule — and is inert until you do. The organisational actions, which are reversible, happen directly:
creating a subcategory, enabling a category, tagging a product, grouping products into a collection.

## What it can do

| Action | How it works |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Issue a gift card** | Prepared as a draft. Nothing is sent to the customer until you approve. |
| **Create a promotion** | Prepared as a draft, in one of the four promotion types. Live once you approve. |
| **Create a dynamic-pricing rule** | Prepared as a draft, targeting a category, product, collection, or customer segment. Takes effect once you approve. Available on plans that include dynamic pricing. |
| **Fix a search that is not working** | Prepared as a draft rule that shows particular products first for a given search, pushes them down, or nudges them up. It reads which searches shoppers run, how often, and how those searches convert, so it can point at a busy search that converts well below your usual rate. A rule only reorders products the search already found — it can never surface something the shopper's words did not match. Takes effect once you approve. Once your storefront reports which result a shopper clicked, each rule also shows how many shoppers clicked what it features and how many of those went on to add to a cart. |
| **Organise your catalogue** | Reviews your categories and subcategories and tidies them: creates a subcategory (nesting it under the right parent rather than adding a redundant one), moves a subcategory to where it belongs, re-files a mis-categorised product, and enables categories that match what you sell. |
| **Tag products** | Sets merchandising tags. |
| **Build and manage a product collection** | Groups products into a curated collection (e.g. "Summer Picks", "Under $50", or your dormant/slow-moving stock — it can read which products haven't sold) that your storefront can feature, and adds or removes members of an existing collection. The members are exactly the products it lists; it's created live, and shows on the homepage unless you say otherwise. |
| **Write a blog post** | Writes a real article — a buying guide, a new-arrivals piece — around products you actually sell, with a summary and search title and description filled in. Saved as a draft under CMS; it goes live when you publish it. |
| **Create a lookbook** | Builds a shoppable page around a theme: a styled scene featuring the products you named, with a clickable hotspot placed on each one it can find in the picture. Saved as a draft — check the positions, swap in your own photography if you prefer, then publish. |
| **Design a banner or cover** | Creates or redraws the desktop and mobile artwork for a promotion, collection, blog post or lookbook you already have. Anything it creates gets artwork automatically, so this is for changing what's there. A post or lookbook cover is treated as editorial artwork — no price, no discount, no call-to-action. Uses media credits. |
| **Suggest reorders** | Surfaces a ranked list of what to restock — a recommendation, not an order. |
| **Draft an advertising campaign** | For whichever ad platform your store has connected: the campaign, its budget guidance and several versions of the ad copy, drawn from what is actually selling, what has stopped moving, and any promotion you are running. It checks the platform's current advertising guidance before writing. Nothing goes live and nothing is spent until you publish it. To advertise a promotion it uses the real discount and its end date — it never invents an offer. If your store has not confirmed where its ads should send people, or the ad account is not serving, it says so, because the campaign cannot run until that is fixed. |
| **Set up an ad experiment** | Turns a set of ad versions into a comparison and reports which is winning. A winner is only called once enough people have seen every version **and** the difference is large enough to be real. Promoting it pauses the losing ads, so it respects the same approval threshold as any other change that spends money. |
| **Write a custom extension** | An integration with something the platform does not natively support, or automation beyond a standing workflow, generated as ready code and grounded in the live API — including a look at the provider's current documentation. It is not installed and does not run: you open it in Settings → Extensions, read it, and save it. Where a standing workflow would do the job, the assistant suggests that instead, since it is simpler to see and change. |

Base product prices are off limits — the assistant only ever touches dynamic-pricing *rules*, never a
product's own price. Anything a plan doesn't include, or your role isn't permitted to do, it won't offer.

Written content needs your storefront URL set first (CMS → Settings). The product links in a post are
built against it, so without one they would only resolve on your own site and break as soon as the post is
shared — the assistant says so and stops rather than writing a post that falls apart.

A lookbook hotspot claims a product is *at that point in that picture*, so it is only ever placed
from what is actually visible in the scene. A product the assistant cannot find in the frame is left
off and reported by name, rather than given a hotspot at an approximate position. If a scene cannot
be produced at all, the lookbook is still created, with your products confirmed against the
catalogue and a plain note that they are not placed yet.

## Approving a draft

Every draft is decided on **Suggestions**, near the top of the sidebar beside Dashboard. It appears there
only when something is waiting, with a count beside it, and it is available on every plan.

Three tabs — **Waiting for you**, **Approved** and **Rejected** — each filterable by type: gift cards,
promotions, pricing rules, search rules, wholesale quotes and credit limits. Every entry shows what is
being proposed, the reasoning behind it, when it was prepared and when it expires.

Approving a gift card is the moment it issues and the customer is notified; approving a promotion or a rule
is the moment it goes live. **Rejecting keeps the record** — it moves to the Rejected tab rather than
disappearing, so you can see what was suggested and declined, and you can always ask for it again. A
suggestion nobody decides eventually expires on its own, which is counted separately: expiring is not the
same as saying no.

You can also approve a suggestion right in the chat when the assistant proposes it, and its launcher shows
a count when any are waiting. Opening Gift Cards, Marketing or Pricing while something of that kind is
waiting shows a short notice offering to take you there — once a visit, and the lists themselves stay free
of drafts.

An advertising campaign the assistant drafts is not here: campaigns are reviewed under
**Integrations → Advertising**, where campaigns live.

<Note>
  Approving an AI suggestion is its own permission, separate from the pages themselves. A store owner can
  grant a teammate — an admin, a marketer — the authority to approve suggestions without changing anything
  else about what they can do. The check is enforced on the server, not just hidden in the interface.
</Note>

## What it aims at

A suggestion is only as good as what it is pointed at, so the assistant reads what shoppers wanted and
did not buy — not only what sold.

- **Carts** — which products sit in baskets that have gone quiet, and what they are worth. A cart whose
  owner has since bought that product is not counted; they did not abandon anything.
- **Abandoned checkouts** — who reached the last step and stopped. The strongest signal in the group,
  because the shopper had already decided.
- **Wishlists** — what has been saved for later, the longest-lived signal of the three.
- **Search outcomes** — what shoppers searched for, which product they clicked, and whether that led to
  a basket, a checkout or a purchase.

The last one matters because it names *where* a search failed, and the three answers need different
fixes: clicked but never added to a basket points at the product page or the price; added but never
reached checkout points at delivery cost or stock; reached checkout but never paid points at payment.

A promotion aimed at what shoppers are actually trying to buy beats one aimed at what merely sells
slowly. And a product that is wanted but **out of stock** is a restocking decision — the assistant will
not propose a discount on something nobody can buy.

## Margin protection

Every value the assistant proposes is in your store's currency, and every money action protects your margin.
It reads each product's cost to make sure a discount never sells below cost, and it prefers higher-margin or
slow-moving stock.

When too many products have no cost price set, the margin figure it would be checking against is
itself unreliable, so it declines to prepare any gift card, promotion, or pricing rule and points you
to **Inventory → Bulk Edit** to fill in the missing costs. The reversible catalogue actions stay
available in the meantime.

## Dynamic workflows

A request in conversation runs once and ends there. A **dynamic workflow** is the same intent kept
running: ask for something ongoing — "keep my featured collection showing what's actually selling",
"every week, check for slow stock" — and it becomes a rule that re-evaluates on a schedule, and
also as soon as something it depends on changes in the store: a product selling out, new products
arriving, a burst of orders.

They are dynamic rather than fixed: each evaluation re-reads the store's current figures and decides
whether anything warrants doing, so the outcome follows the business rather than a predetermined
sequence — the same workflow features different products as the sales change. This is what
distinguishes them from [event-driven automations](/workflows/webhooks-automation), where a defined
action follows a specific event. An event can wake a workflow early, but what the workflow then does
is still decided from the store's figures at that moment.

A burst of related changes wakes a workflow once rather than once per change, a workflow's own
changes never wake it again, and the scheduled evaluation still runs, which is how conditions that no
single change announces — stock that has stopped selling, a collection member that has gone stale —
are noticed.

A workflow observes the same approval boundary as a conversation. Reversible merchandising runs
unattended: rotating a collection to current best sellers, retiring members that stopped selling,
tagging what sells. Anything touching money prepares a draft and waits — promotions and clearances,
gift cards for customers who pass a spending threshold in a window, and markdown pricing rules over
stock that stopped moving.

Search merchandising works the same way on a schedule. A workflow keeps watching which searches
convert badly and drafts a rule when one is worth fixing, so it follows whichever search is failing
this season rather than the one that was failing when the rule was written. It proposes only when a
search is both busy and converting well below your baseline, and only ever features a product that
search already matches. A busy search that returns nothing at all is reported as a stocking decision
instead, since a reordering rule has nothing to reorder. Where there is no cost data to choose by
margin it says so rather than guessing, and it will not draft a second rule while one is still
waiting for you.

The money templates carry per-run limits. A promotion template drafts one promotion. A gift-card
template drafts one card per qualifying customer, each of which is a liability the merchant owes, so
a single run is capped on both the number of recipients and their combined face value; a rule
matching more customers than the cap allows stops and reports that rather than drafting an arbitrary
subset, and a cooldown keeps the same customer from being rewarded again on the following run. A
markdown rule is scoped to the specific products it identified rather than the whole catalogue, is
drafted switched off, and leaves only one draft outstanding at a time, so a weekly cadence cannot
stack duplicates while the merchant decides. Every one of these limits is enforced in the database,
not in the prompt.

### Content on a schedule

Writing is a standing intent too: ask for a post or a lookbook on a recurring basis and each run
produces a new one — a real piece, built around products you actually sell, with a cover image,
saved as a draft under CMS. Nothing is published for you; it is your public voice, so you publish it.

Give the rule either a fixed topic or a **category**. With a category it chooses its subject afresh
every run from whatever shoppers are most drawn to there, so the piece follows real demand instead of
repeating something you decided months ago. If nothing in that category drew interest that week, it
writes nothing and tells you why. These run weekly at most, and a rule pauses itself once three of its
drafts are waiting unread — publishing or discarding some starts it again.

### Seasons and days

Any workflow can be scoped to part of the year and to particular weekdays — promotions, collections,
tagging, gift cards, pricing and content alike, not only the writing ones.

A **season** recurs. Set up a Black Friday rule for November or a Valentine's rule for February once,
and it wakes every year on its own; out of season it is skipped rather than switched off, so it comes
back without you re-enabling it, keeping its name, settings and history instead of being deleted and
rebuilt each year. A window can wrap the year end, for a mid-December to early-January run.

**Weekdays** pin a rule to the days you mean. On its own a weekly rule is an interval, so it drifts by
a day or so each time; naming Friday makes it land on Friday and stay there.

They combine:

| You want | How it is set up |
| :--- | :--- |
| A lookbook every Friday in December, featuring whatever is most in demand in Wearables | Weekly · Fridays · 1–31 December · category Wearables |
| A clearance draft every year in the first week of January | Weekly · 1–7 January |
| A post about new arrivals every week, all year | Weekly · no season |
| Featured rotated to current best sellers every Monday | Weekly · Mondays |

Dynamic workflows are managed at **Settings → Workflows**, which is also where each one can be paused
or removed. Alongside the configuration is the record of every run — what it did, and the figures
behind the decision, so a change always has an explanation attached rather than appearing unannounced.

A workflow that fails repeatedly switches itself off and says so, rather than retrying indefinitely.

## Autonomous workflows

A workflow still begins as a request. Autonomous mode removes that step: the assistant watches how the
store is performing and keeps the set of standing rules current on its own — setting up rules worth
running, retuning ones whose thresholds have drifted from the business, and retiring ones that have
stopped producing anything.

It is opt-in per store, at **Settings → Workflows**, and it changes who maintains the rules rather
than what a rule may do. **The approval boundary is unchanged.** Discounts, gift cards, pricing rules
and search rules are still prepared as drafts and still wait for a person, under the same permission
and on the same pages. What becomes automatic is the upkeep, not the spending.

Three constraints hold it in place, and each is enforced in Galactic Core's own code rather than left
to the assistant's judgement — they sit alongside the wider set of platform
[guardrails](/how-gc-works/guardrails):

- **A rule a person paused stays paused.** Pausing is an instruction, not an untidy state to be
  corrected, so it is never restarted automatically.
- **A rule that has ever acted is never retired.** Retirement is reserved for rules that have produced
  nothing across a sustained run of evaluations, counted from the run log rather than asserted.
- **Nothing happens without a record.** Every adjustment writes a run entry carrying the figures it
  was based on, beside the entries for the actions themselves — and the two are distinguishable, so
  "what changed in my shop" and "what changed about my rules" remain separate questions.

Running in the background consumes AI credits, which is why the same setting establishes how they are
replenished: an amount, the balance that triggers it, and a ceiling for the month. A payment that does
not go through is reported by email, and a second failure stops the attempts rather than re-presenting
the card. Without replenishment the assistant simply stops maintaining the rules; the rules themselves
carry on running as they were.

Where a store has a marketing goal recorded, the assistant reads it — what the merchant said they are
optimising for, and until when — so its decisions follow that objective. Where there is none, which is
the ordinary case, nothing changes.

### Product pages

One workflow arrives with every store rather than being asked for. It looks for products whose
search title and description are empty and writes them, so those pages can be found by search
engines. A product added or edited without them is picked up shortly afterwards, and a pass once a
month works through anything still outstanding. It works from the product itself and from the terms shoppers on that store have searched for,
it only ever fills a gap rather than replacing wording a merchant wrote, and it starts with the pages
that have nothing at all. A catalogue larger than a month's budget is covered over several months,
emptiest pages first.

### Seasons

A store's seasons depend on where its customers actually are, so they are read from real storefront
traffic rather than assumed. A store selling into the United States meets Thanksgiving and Black
Friday; one selling into Britain meets Boxing Day instead; a store selling into both meets all three.
Where there is too little traffic to say, nothing is proposed rather than something guessed.

Each season carries the lead time an offer needs, so a rule is set up when there is still time to
approve and stock it — not on the day. Seasons that suit particular catalogues are proposed only to
the stores that sell those things. Dates that move from year to year, like Thanksgiving, are resolved
to the exact day.

What arrives is a standing rule rather than a finished promotion: it recurs each year, and what it
prepares still waits for approval like any other offer.

### Advertising and merchandising

The two sides read each other. A clearance rule leaves out products that advertising is driving sales
to — otherwise the same merchant pays for the click and then discounts the sale it produced — and
records how many it held back. In the other direction, the advertising side sees which promotions are
live or scheduled, which markdown rules are active, and that changes are waiting on the merchant's
approval.

### Rules that arrive with a store

A store set up by an agency can start with standing rules already running, chosen by that agency for
the merchants it serves. They are ordinary workflows from the moment the store exists: the merchant
pauses, retunes or removes them like any other, and what they change stays changed. Defining that set
is described under [agencies](/agencies).

### Limits

The number of standing rules a store may have, how long the run record is kept, and how many
consecutive failures switch a rule off are all set per store, under **Settings → Workflows → Settings**.
Each starts from a default appropriate to the plan and can be adjusted: a catalogue spanning dozens of
categories and a focused one have different needs, and the ceiling follows the plan for any store that
has not chosen its own.

## Where the decision sits

The assistant never issues a gift card, activates a promotion, or turns on a pricing rule on its own.
The margin check and the approval step are enforced in Galactic Core's own code rather than left to
the assistant's judgement, so a prompt cannot talk its way past either; they are part of a wider set
of platform [guardrails](/how-gc-works/guardrails). Every action it takes is recorded in your
activity log under its own name, so an AI-assisted change is as traceable as a manual one.

What it can see is bounded the same way. The assistant reads your store through a **business schema**
— a fixed, named layer of business concepts — rather than querying your data directly, with the store
bound before any concept is read, and margin arriving as a computed figure rather than as the cost
behind it. That is how it can refuse a discount for selling below cost without the cost itself ever
entering the conversation. The arrangement is described in full under
[the business schema the assistant reads through](/how-gc-works/guardrails#the-business-schema-the-assistant-reads-through).

## Shopping assistants: the Agent API

The in-app assistant works for the merchant. The Agent API serves the other side of the counter: an AI
shopping assistant helping a shopper buy from a store — the store's own chat widget, a voice interface, a
generated storefront, or a third-party assistant. Galactic Core supplies the tools rather than the
assistant. The developer brings the model and the conversation design; the tools supply what a model
cannot know or must not guess — live stock, the price a shopper is actually charged, the promotion that
applies, shipping and tax for an address, the return window — and a checkout the shopper confirms
themselves.

The endpoints live under `/v1/agent/*` and are available in the SDK as `client.agent`; the
[Agent reference](/sdk/api-reference/classes/AgentService) documents every method with real responses.
Every response shares one envelope: the result in `data`, and an `evidence` array naming the operation and
field each figure was read from, so an assistant can cite a number and a reviewer can trace it. Most tools
need only a publishable key.

### Finding a store

A store publishes `agent.json` at `/v1/feeds/{store}/agent.json`: the API's base address, a production
publishable key dedicated to assistants, links to the tool manifest and the OpenAPI specification, the
store's feeds, and `checkout.human_confirmation_required: true`. No key is needed to read it. A
storefront serves the same document at `/.well-known/agentic-commerce.json` by forwarding that path, and
the store's [`llms.txt`](/sdk/api-reference/classes/FeedsService#getstorellmstxt) links to it, so an
assistant that reads either can start calling the Agent API without being configured for the store in
advance.

The descriptor is published by default, except by a wholesale store, which publishes it once the
merchant turns it on. The merchant controls it from **Settings → Agentic Commerce**, and turning it off
also deactivates its key. A store on a trial does not publish one. The
[Feeds reference](/sdk/api-reference/classes/FeedsService#getstoreagentdescriptor) shows the document.

### The tools

| Group | Tools |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Discovery** | A manifest of every tool with the JSON Schema of its request and response; constraint search (price, category, attributes, stock) where each result lists which constraints it met; a product dossier with each variant's live stock and price, specifications, review summary, shipping and return window; side-by-side comparison with specifications aligned; a fit check that separates constraints met, missed, and unknown because the attribute is not published; alternatives by similarity, price, stock or matching specification. |
| **Cart and decision** | A quote giving the landed total of a basket — promotion, shipping and tax for the address, priced in the same order as an order; cart drafts kept apart from the shopper's live cart, re-quoted on every read and added to the live cart on request; insights on a cart such as price changes, the gap to free shipping and complementary items. |
| **The shopper's own data** | Order status with the next allowed actions, a return lodged for one of the shopper's own orders, and wishlist availability and price drops. Personal context from the shopper's own orders — sizes, price band, reorder suggestions — and standing instructions are available only after the shopper grants consent, which is separate from email marketing consent. |
| **Checkout intents** | Create, read, confirm and cancel — described below. |
| **Standing instructions** | Create, list and cancel a shopper's instruction to reorder on a schedule or buy when an item is back in stock or reaches a price — described below. |
| **Store** | Return policy and reasons, shipping regions and base fees, accepted payment methods and currencies. |

A specification the merchant has not published is reported as absent, never inferred, and a basket line
that cannot be bought is flagged rather than dropped. No tool exposes cost, margin, or what other shoppers
are doing.

### Two ways to pay for the model

| Mode | Who pays for inference |
| :--- | :--- |
| **The developer's own model** | The developer, with their own model provider. The assistant calls only the deterministic tools, which cost what any storefront request costs. |
| **Hosted helpers** | The store, from its **Agent Compute credits**. Two helpers run a model: one turns a request in plain language into the constraints search accepts, the other drafts a priced, stock-checked cart from an intent and a budget, choosing only among in-stock search results. |

A helper call runs entirely on the store's side — the model, the tool calls it makes, and the pricing and
stock checks behind its answer — and returns a finished result, so the calling application holds no model
provider key and runs no model of its own for that step. The conversation itself, and when to call each
tool, remain the application's.

Every tool other than the two helpers is deterministic. The helpers are off until the merchant turns them
on, which requires automatic recharge of the credit balance with a monthly limit. Each call is debited at
its true cost and reports the amount; a failed call keeps nothing. When the helpers cannot run — not
enabled, paused, or out of credits — they say so, and every deterministic tool keeps working. A server
holding the store's secret key reads the balance, the recharge state and each month's helper spend with
`getUsage`.

### Checkout intents

An assistant never places an order or takes payment. It creates a **checkout intent**: the basket is
priced for the shipping address, the price is frozen, the stock is held for 30 minutes, and a single-use
confirmation token is returned. The shopper's approval is collected in one of two ways. By default the
shopper reviews the frozen total in the developer's interface and approves it; the application then
confirms the intent with the token, alongside the shopper's credential or, for a guest, their contact
details. Alternatively the assistant asks for **hosted confirmation** and receives a link to the store's
own confirmation page, which it gives to the shopper. The page shows the frozen quote, takes the
shopper's contact details, confirms the intent, and passes payment to the store's payment provider — a
Stripe Checkout redirect, a Paystack popup, PayPal buttons, or a custom provider's redirect — so an
assistant without a checkout interface of its own can still complete a purchase.

Confirmation prices the basket again. If anything the shopper would pay has changed — a line price, the
discount, shipping, tax or the total — nothing is placed, and the response carries the new quote so the
shopper can decide again. Otherwise the order is placed with payment pending, using the held stock, and
payment proceeds through the store's normal payment flow. The token is stored only as a hash, works once,
and a wrong one reveals nothing about the intent. An intent left unconfirmed expires and releases its
stock; one the shopper declines can be cancelled at once.

### Limits on a publishable key

A checkout intent holds stock, and a publishable key can be read from any storefront, so intents
created with one are bounded: 60 new intents an hour from one address, 5 waiting for confirmation at
once per address (per shopper when a shopper credential is sent), 10 units of one item per intent for
a guest and 25 for a signed-in shopper, and guests together may hold at most half of an item's stock.
Past a limit the intent is refused with `409 hold_limit_reached` and the limit in `details.limit`, or
`429 rate_limited` with `Retry-After`. A secret key, used from the store's own server, is bound only by
its plan.

### Returns

`getOrderStatus` lists `request_return` among an order's allowed actions while the order is inside the
store's return window, and `requestReturn` lodges it for the shopper — the whole order, or chosen lines
and quantities, with a reason. The return arrives in the store's returns queue as pending, exactly as
one lodged from the storefront would; the store reviews it and decides the refund, and the shopper is
emailed that it was received.

### Standing instructions

A shopper can leave an instruction for later: reorder an item every so many days, or buy it when it is
back in stock or reaches a price. Every instruction carries a maximum unit price, a quantity of at most
10 and an expiry of at most a year; a reorder runs every 7 to 365 days, at most 12 times, and the other
two fire once. A shopper can keep 10 with one store.

When an instruction's condition holds, Galactic Core prepares a checkout intent at the price it found
and sends the shopper a link to the store's confirmation page, by email under the store's own name and
in the `agent.mandate.triggered` webhook event. The link stays open for 48 hours. The shopper confirms
and pays on that page; nothing is bought without them. Instructions need the shopper's credential and
personalisation consent, and withdrawing consent pauses them.

### Events and attribution

Checkout intents emit three [webhook events](/webhooks): `agent.checkout_intent.created` when an assistant
prepares one, `agent.checkout_intent.confirmed` when the shopper confirms and the order exists, and
`agent.checkout_intent.expired` when one lapses unconfirmed. A standing instruction that fires emits
`agent.mandate.triggered`, carrying the `confirmation_url` the shopper confirms at. An order placed from a confirmed intent is
recorded as an agent order: its `order.created` event carries `source_channel: "agent"` (an order placed
directly through `POST /v1/orders` carries `"storefront"`) and `agent_client_ref`, which identifies the API key or the connected application
that created the intent.

The store also keeps a record of assistant activity for its own merchant, separate from its storefront
analytics: the words of each search and how many products matched, the products an assistant opened or
compared, cart drafts, and how each checkout intent ended. These records carry no shopper identity. The
merchant reads them through the in-app assistant, which can answer what shopping assistants asked for,
which searches found nothing in the catalogue, and which products they looked at most.
